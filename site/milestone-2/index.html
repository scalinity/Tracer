Tracer

Tracer finds specimens that are lost or stalled between collection and receipt, and points the right person to them while there is still time to recover them.

Karissa and Daniel, CAI 5724.

This page describes Tracer as if it were already running in a hospital microbiology lab.

Walk through one run
1

Project update

Since Milestone 1, Karissa’s experience at the bench shifted our focus from specimens that degrade during ordinary delay to specimens that are lost or stalled, because stability windows run from 24 hours to 7 days while transport takes minutes to hours.

The stability clock runs from collection to receipt in the laboratory. The unobserved interval is the same stretch, and it includes the time a specimen waits on the laboratory counter before it is scanned in.

In plain terms

The problem we are solving changed. Specimens rarely run out of time on an ordinary trip, so the real risk is one that disappears or gets stuck along the way. Tracer is now built around finding those.

2

Objective

Tracer detects specimens that are lost or stalled between collection and receipt early, estimates where each one most likely is, and directs the right person to it before recollection becomes necessary.

Today the interval between collection and receipt is unobserved, so a specimen that never arrives produces no signal until someone notices it is missing.

In plain terms

A missing specimen makes no noise. Tracer listens to that silence and speaks up when a specimen should have turned up by now, along with where to look.

3

Form factor

Tracer is a background service that continuously reads specimen events from the laboratory information system and carrier events from the pneumatic tube system.

It shows a live board at the specimen receiving area that ranks every outstanding specimen by urgency, sends alerts to the receiving tech’s workstation, and keeps a timeline for each specimen with every scan, the time spent on each leg, and where the specimen most likely is now.

In plain terms

A background service is software that runs all the time without anyone opening it. There is nothing to launch or log in to. Tracer shows up in three places: a screen at receiving, alerts at the workstation, and a history for each specimen.

4

User interaction

The primary users are the receiving and plating assistants and techs, who are usually the first to notice a missing specimen today. Supervisors are the escalation tier, and collecting units are contacted when a specimen was never sent.

Tracer runs on every specimen and needs no input to start. When a specimen needs attention, it sends an alert with a short search brief to the receiving tech and posts it to the live board. The tech records where the specimen was found or that it was not, which closes the alert.

In plain terms

Nobody has to remember to use it. When something looks wrong, it taps the right person on the shoulder. Their one-tap answer closes the loop and becomes a record of what happened.

5

Context, before and after

A missing specimen produces no signal, so it is noticed late and indirectly. The search that follows is manual and escalates in steps.

  1. No signal when a specimen stops moving.
  2. Someone notices late, and indirectly.
  3. Staff search for it by hand.
  4. After several failed attempts, a supervisor is brought in.
  5. If the specimen is declared lost, a hospital-wide lost-specimen alert goes out. Every department searches its area and empties stored pneumatic tubes.

Tracer runs on every specimen all the time, so there is no decision to make about using it. The search starts early and starts in the right place.

  1. An abnormally long dwell on a specific leg is flagged within minutes to hours.
  2. The alert names the most likely location and the time left before recollection would be required.
  3. The search is targeted at that location first.
  4. The tech records the outcome, which closes the alert.
  5. Most losses are resolved before a hospital-wide alert would be needed, and fewer patients need to be recollected.
In plain terms

Today the search starts late and wide. With Tracer it starts early and narrow, at the one place the specimen most likely is.

6

System inputs and outputs

A single run is one assessment of one specimen at one moment. It is triggered by a new scan or pneumatic tube event, or by a periodic check while the specimen is between scans.

Inputs
  • Specimen type and tests ordered, since an anaerobic culture shortens the window
  • Collection time and every later scan timestamp, from the laboratory information system
  • Carrier dispatch and arrival events, from the pneumatic tube system
  • The current time and the current receiving volume in the lab
Outputs
  • The specimen’s status
  • Its most likely location, with ranked alternatives
  • The expected time of its next scan
  • Time remaining before recollection would be required
  • When warranted, a prioritized alert with a short search brief, sent to the receiving tech’s workstation and the live board

Everything except the alert updates the live board and the specimen’s timeline. The outcome the tech records is logged with the alert.

In plain terms

Each run is Tracer taking one look at one specimen and asking three questions: is it on track, where is it, and how long do we have. It asks again on every new scan, and on a timer while nothing happens, because silence is the signal.

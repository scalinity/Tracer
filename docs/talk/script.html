The Tracer talk, November 18

The in-class presentation for CAI 5724: the run of show, the slides and every line we say. It runs 17:00, 2 minutes over the 15-minute slot by choice, so there is time to say what Tracer is, what it is built to fix, and how we built and tested it.

Words in brackets are stage directions, not lines. The word counts assume about 130 words a minute, a classroom pace with pauses.

The silent tube

Karissa

A tube leaves the ward. Nothing beeps, and nothing gets logged. Nobody knows yet if it's late. So, raise your hand if you'd start to worry after fifteen minutes. Thirty? An hour? Hold on to that number, because that's the guess Tracer has to make, for every specimen, all day.

0:30 to 1:30 Slide 2

Why it matters

KarissaThe room is: listening

On screen Three facts. 61.9% of errors in one stat laboratory's audit happened before analysis (Carraro and Plebani, Clin Chem 2007). Unpreserved urine at room temperature: a 60-minute window in our policy table, from the IDSA and ASM 2024 guide. A missed window usually means a new specimen: another needle stick or lumbar puncture, and hours of delay.

Karissa

Most lab errors don't happen during testing. In one stat laboratory's audit, about sixty-two percent of errors happened before the specimen was ever analyzed. And a microbiology specimen is on a clock. Unpreserved urine at room temperature has about an hour before bacteria overgrow and the colony count stops meaning anything. Spinal fluid has to come straight to the lab, and it must not be refrigerated. When a specimen misses its window, it's often rejected and has to be collected again. For the patient, that's another needle stick or another lumbar puncture, and hours of delay. And the lab has no record of where a specimen is between collection and receipt, so a missing one often isn't noticed until someone calls asking for the result.

[About 125 words.]

1:30 to 2:00 Slide 3

The missing data

DanielThe room is: listening

On screen Two dots on a line, "Collected" and "Result filed", with nothing between them. Median 80.1 hours apart, across 1,291 specimens in the public MIMIC-IV demo extract. No receipt scan, no transport record.

Daniel

So why not learn this from hospital data? The best-known public one, MIMIC-IV, stamps a microbiology specimen twice: when it's collected and when the result is filed. Those stamps are a median of eighty hours apart, with nothing in between. There's no field for the journey at all. So we generated our own data, and every number you'll see is an assumption we can change.

[About 65 words.]

2:00 to 2:45 Slide 4

Meet Tracer

DanielThe room is: listening

On screen "An early warning for specimens in transit." Like package tracking with no scans along the way. What it reads: when it was collected, what kind it is and where from, and how long it can wait. What it gives the lab, every 5 minutes: who is at risk, ranked; where each one likely is; and who should go and look. No new hardware: only the scans the lab already records.

Daniel

With that data, we built Tracer. Think of package tracking with no scans along the way. You know when it left, and how long trips like it usually take, so at first, silence means nothing. But the longer it stays quiet past normal, and the closer it gets to its own deadline, the more you should worry. Tracer does that for every specimen the lab is waiting on, every five minutes, using only the scans the lab already records. It ranks them by risk, says where each one most likely is, and alerts someone who can go look, while there's still time.

[About 100 words.]

2:45 to 3:30 Slide 5

What Tracer is built to fix

KarissaThe room is: listening

On screen Today and with Tracer, side by side. Today a missing specimen makes no noise; Tracer scores each one every 5 minutes. Today it is found when someone calls for it; Tracer alerts while there is still time. Today one time limit covers every specimen; Tracer judges each by its own window. Tracking tags would need readers in every hallway; Tracer uses only the scans the lab records. The aim: fewer repeat collections and quicker results for patients, and less time chasing specimens for the lab.

Karissa

So what does that change in the lab? Today, the lab's screens only show what's been scanned, so a missing specimen stays silent until someone calls asking for the result. A fixed time limit is too late for urine and too early for a swab. And tracking tags would need a tag on every tube and a reader in every hallway. Tracer is built to close those gaps with data the lab already has. The aim is fewer repeat collections and quicker results for the patient, and less time chasing for the lab.

[About 90 words.]

3:30 to 4:15 Slide 6

How Tracer works

DanielThe room is: listening

On screen "Silence is evidence." Every 5 minutes, for every specimen not yet received, three questions, each with a picture. How unusual is this silence: a dotted silence running past where trips like it usually arrive. Where is it most likely: the unit, the tube and the station, each sized by how likely it is. Is there still time to act: the time left between an alert and the window closing. Then: an alert, routed to the role that can check that place. Switch to the 3D bench for the second half.

Daniel

So how does it know? Silence is evidence. Under the hood, Tracer asks three questions. How unusual is this much silence, for this kind of specimen? Where is it most likely to be right now: still on the unit, in the tube, or sitting at the tube station? And if we alert now, is there still time to act before its window closes? [Switch to the 3D bench.] This is a tube bench built from the same model. The light along the tube is what Tracer infers. [Press T to show the truth.] And this carrier is where the specimen really was. It's a small model you can read, not a black box.

[About 100 words.]

11:45 to 12:45 Slide 9 Hands-on

Why this design

DanielThe room is: dragging the slider

On screen First the demo's "Set the threshold" section on the projector, while the room drags it on their phones. Then the numbers slide: median warning at 6 alerts per 8-hour shift on the held-out synthetic runs. The plan's time-limit rule 30 minutes, a rule that alerts at a fixed share of the window 45, Tracer 55 (45 with equal tier weights).

Daniel

That trade is the threshold. Scroll down to Set the threshold and drag it both ways. Lower, and Tracer warns earlier but alerts on specimens that would have arrived anyway. Higher, fewer alerts, less warning. So we judge Tracer by minutes of warning at a number of alerts a shift can handle, not by accuracy. [Switch to the numbers.] At six alerts a shift, Tracer gave fifty-five minutes of median warning, against thirty for a simple time limit. But a rule that alerts once a set share of the window has passed gets forty-five. With equal weights, Tracer only ties it. Most of the gain comes from using the window.

[About 105 words, over the room dragging the slider.]

12:45 to 13:30 Slide 10

How we built it

Karissa, then DanielThe room is: listening

On screen "How we built and tested Tracer," in two rows. The data: briefs drawn by code (12,000 specimens, faults planted on purpose), then Claude writes the scans (each trail and its hidden true path), then a validator checks them (nine checks, 11,967 kept and frozen, $2.16 in all). The model: a three-part survival model is fit on 24 runs (normal trip times by type, hour and priority; trips that run far longer but arrive; specimens that never arrive), it is tested on 6 runs it never saw (2,378 specimens, 100 missed windows), and its numbers go to the demo you used (two files, no server). The hidden truth goes only to the test, never to the model.

Karissa

Here's how we built it. We couldn't find public data with these scans, so we wrote our own. Code draws twelve thousand specimens from assumed timings and my policy table. Claude writes each one's scans and its hidden true path, and a validator keeps the ones that pass nine checks.

[About 50 words.]

Daniel

Then a small survival model learns from twenty-four of the thirty runs: how long a normal trip takes for each kind of specimen and time of day, how often a trip runs far longer and still arrives, and how often one never does. We test it on the other six, and only that test sees the hidden truth.

[About 60 words.]

Some honest limits. Tracer only watches time and place, so it can't see a mislabeled or leaking specimen. Prepared answers

The suggested speaker is in brackets. Either of us can take any of them. Keep each answer to two or three sentences, and say what we don't know.

  1. 1 What if a scanner just misses a scan?

    Daniel

    We didn't plant missed scans in the data, so that's a gap. A missed receipt scan would look like a late specimen, so Tracer would raise its risk and someone would check, which costs one alert.

  2. 2 Why not just alert on everything after 30 minutes?

    Daniel

    That's close to the simple rule we compared against. It ignores the window, and the windows in our table run from 60 minutes for unpreserved urine to days for a swab in transport medium, so one time limit is too late for some and far too early for others.

  3. 3 What would someone actually do with an alert?

    Karissa

    Go to the place Tracer names, like the tube station or the collecting unit, and look for it. Who gets which alert is an assumption we made, and a real lab would set it.

  4. 4 Isn't it circular to test on data you generated?

    Daniel

    Partly, yes. The data and the model share our assumptions, so a good result shows the method behaves as designed, not that it works in a real lab. That's why the next step is a real log with receipt scans.

  5. 5 Why a simple model and not deep learning?

    Daniel

    A lab scientist can read its numbers: how long a normal trip takes, how often a specimen stalls and still arrives, how often it never does. A gradient-boosted challenger is planned and hasn't been run.

  6. 6 How often is it right about where the specimen is?

    Daniel

    At 30 minutes of silence, the true place is in its top three about 88% of the time and its first guess about 55%, on synthetic data. So an alert says something is off more reliably than it says where.

  7. 7 How many false alarms?

    Daniel

    Plenty, by design. At 6 alerts a shift about a third of all specimens get an alert, and most of them arrive in time. Its risk scores are only slightly sharper than guessing the average rate, so the gain is in timing, not in sorting.

  8. 8 Why does the clock stop at receipt?

    Karissa

    Where a window ends is a clinical judgement, and receipt in the lab is the standard measure. That's what the policy table uses, so a delay at the bench after receipt is outside what Tracer watches.

  9. 9 Is any real patient or hospital data in this?

    Karissa

    No. We checked a public 100-patient MIMIC-IV extract and set it aside because it describes intensive care, not a general lab. No workplace data is used anywhere.

  10. 10 How did you use AI to build it?

    Daniel

    Claude Code, a coding agent, wrote the code and tests under rules I set, and I made the calls the repo couldn't. A small Claude model wrote the scan trails: 12,000 requested, 11,967 passed a nine-check validator, for about two dollars. It's all in the AI teaming log in the repo.

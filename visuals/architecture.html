In real hospital records the gap is wider still. A public de-identified extract of 1,336 microbiology specimens holds the collection at stage 2 and the final result at stage 8 and nothing whatever in between — no receipt, no accessioning, no setup — and the median distance between those two timestamps is 80 hours, because it contains the incubation. Stages 3 through 7 are unobserved in real data too. That is why every specimen here is simulated.

So what is the actual problem?

Every step on that line leaves a record except one. While a specimen is travelling to the laboratory it is out of contact — nobody can say where it is, and nobody is meant to. That is normal and it is usually fine, because most of them arrive.

The trouble starts when one doesn't. A specimen that goes missing sets off no alarm, because an alarm needs something to happen and nothing has. It surfaces hours later, when a doctor rings to ask where a result is, or a technologist notices an order with no tube against it. By then the sample has often sat too long to be worth testing, so the patient is stuck for another draw and the diagnosis waits another day.

Why does the risk climb when nothing has happened?

Because nothing happening is the information. A urine leaving this ward usually reaches the laboratory in about half an hour. At ten minutes of silence there is nothing to think about. At forty minutes the innocent explanations have mostly run out — the ordinary trip is over and this one is still not here.

So the software is not reacting to an event. It wakes up every five minutes, looks at each specimen still unaccounted for, and asks how unusual that silence is for this kind of sample from this ward at this hour. When the answer gets bad enough that someone could still fix it, it says so, names the most likely place to look, and sends it to the person who can walk over there. Here that happens at 02:54, with 20 minutes of the specimen's useful life still left.

What is the software actually doing?

Four things, in order. It keeps a record card for every specimen that has been collected and not yet finished, and updates the card whenever a scan comes in. It works out, from the laboratory's own history, how long each kind of specimen normally takes — a urine from this ward at two in the morning is a different animal from one from a clinic at noon. Every five minutes it compares each card against that history and turns the answer into a number: how likely is this one to miss its deadline.

Then it decides who, if anyone, hears about it. That last part matters more than it sounds. A system that flags everything gets switched off in a fortnight, so it is built to stay inside a fixed number of alerts per shift and spend them on the specimens where the harm would be worst.

The one piece that is honestly a guess is the map of where a missing specimen probably is. Nothing records that anywhere, so it can only be tested against a simulation, and the page says so wherever it appears.

What on this page is illustrative

The worked example is computed from the models in the architecture, but it runs on invented numbers and a scripted ending. The distinction matters, because the Week 15 demo has to be driven by the real prototype rather than by a page that resembles one.

computed from the design
  • The risk curve, from the mixture-survival model: a normal component, a stalled component, and a mass that never completes.
  • The location probabilities, from a hidden semi-Markov forward filter conditioned on no scan having arrived.
  • The alert moment, as the first five-minute tick past the operating point, and the lead time that follows from it.
illustrative, and still to close
  • Every transit parameter is invented. The 60-minute window is the policy table's, for unpreserved urine at room temperature; the transit times are not fitted to anything.
  • The recovery is scripted. Nothing decided this specimen would be found at 03:06.
  • The page evaluates the equation directly with hand-set parameters; it does not load a model fitted to an event log.
  • Build statuses are maintained by hand in the page source.
